At a company that builds industrial furnaces, the technical offer sheet is prepared by an engineer. They read the customer's specification, work out operating temperatures, loads, usable dimensions and atmosphere, check that the values hang together and write the sheet, often in the customer's language. For a custom plant that takes days, and a wrong figure in the offer gets paid for on the job.

It is the right work to have AI prepare, on one condition: the engineer still decides.

**In brief:**
- On a custom plant, the technical offer sheet costs an engineer days of work, and a wrong figure in the offer gets paid for on the job.
- AI can pre-fill the specification checklist from the customer's documents and flag missing or inconsistent data; sizing and price stay with the engineer.
- The sheet is generated in the required languages from the checked specifications, which then go into a shared database the next offer can reuse.
- From 20 January 2027 the Machinery Regulation (EU) 2023/1230 applies: instructions in the language set by the Member State concerned and, if digital, available online for at least 10 years.
- The first module of a system like this goes into production in 4 weeks.

## Why the technical offer sheet takes days

An industrial furnace is not picked from a catalogue. Every request arrives in a different shape: a long specification, the customer's internal standard, an email with a few figures and an attachment, sometimes in another language.

Before writing a line, the engineer has to rebuild the specifications:

- **the process**: type of heat treatment, temperatures, cycles, atmosphere;
- **the load**: material, weights, dimensions, required throughput;
- **the plant**: heating type, energy available at the site, space;
- **the customer's requirements**: reference standards, documentation, acceptance tests.

Then they have to check that it all adds up, and that is where errors creep in: a value taken from an outdated revision of the specification, a mixed-up unit of measure, a figure the customer never gave and someone assumed. Every error in the offer becomes a wrong price or a redesign after the order.

And while the engineer writes offers, they are not designing plants.

## What we are building, step by step

For a builder of custom industrial furnaces and heat-treatment plants, we are building a web app that takes the offer from request to final sheet:

1. **Collects the customer's documents** as they arrive, including in other languages.
2. **Pre-fills the specification checklist** with the values found in the documents.
3. **Checks the data**: mandatory fields, units of measure, values that contradict each other. Whatever is missing stays highlighted.
4. **The engineer completes and corrects**, voice commands included, instead of copying data by hand.
5. **Generates the technical offer sheet** in the required languages, from the checked specifications.
6. **Saves the specifications to a shared database**: the next offer for a similar plant does not start from scratch.

The first module goes into production in 4 weeks; the rest follows one release at a time.

## Where AI helps, and where the engineer decides

AI handles the part that eats hours today without adding expertise: reading messy documents, finding the values, putting them into the checklist, writing the same sheet in several languages.

Data checks, on the other hand, are better written as rules, together with the people who design the furnaces: a rule always gives the same answer and can be explained to the customer. ## The languages of the offer, and of the instructions

If you build plants for export, you write offers in several languages, and selling abroad takes localization more than translation, as we explain in [AI for export and market expansion](/en/blog/ai-export-market-expansion/). After the order, languages come back in the machine's documentation, with precise rules.

From 20 January 2027, the [Machinery Regulation (EU) 2023/1230](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2023/1230/oj) applies and replaces Directive 2006/42/EC: machinery placed on the market before that date still follows the Directive, as the [European Commission's machinery page](https://single-market-economy.ec.europa.eu/sectors/mechanical-engineering/machinery_en) notes. Article 10 requires the manufacturer to ensure that:

- instructions for use and safety information are in a language easily understood by users, **as determined by the Member State concerned**;
- if the instructions are digital, they can be downloaded and printed and stay **available online for at least 10 years** after the machine is placed on the market;
- if the user asks at the time of purchase, a paper copy is provided **free of charge within one month**.

The system we are building stops at the offer. But having specifications in an orderly, checked database, already written in several languages, pays off later too: they are the same data that design and documentation start from.

## When you don't need a custom system

Straight talk, before you spend anything:

- **Few offers a year**: if you prepare a few dozen, a good sheet template and a shared checklist will do.
- **Catalogue products**: if the offer is a combination of known variants, you need a configurator, not a system that reads specifications.
- **The rules are not written down anywhere**: if the checks live only in a senior engineer's head, they have to be written down first. We do that together in the Analysis, but budget for the work.

If every offer is a project, specifications arrive in several languages and your engineers spend days copying data, the numbers add up quickly.
